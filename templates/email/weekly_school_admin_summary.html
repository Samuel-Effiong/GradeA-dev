What the Rigor column means

Rigor answers one question: is this teacher asking enough of their students, and is it landing? Every teacher gets a short verdict below, based on the three things we can measure.

Verdict What it means What to do
Stretching students Questions ask students to analyse and judge, and results show they are being pushed without being lost. Nothing — this is the pattern you want.
Demanding questions The questions ask for real thinking, but not enough work has been graded yet to know how students cope. Check back once marking catches up.
Check the marking The questions are hard, yet nearly every student scores top marks. Read a few marked scripts. Marking may be too generous, or answers may be circulating.
Too easy Questions mainly ask students to remember facts, and they score highly. Encourage questions that ask students to explain, compare or justify.
Very hard going Demanding questions and students are scoring low. Students may need more preparation before work at this level.
Struggling on basics Questions only ask for recall, and students still score low. Look at teaching support rather than difficulty.
Balanced / Moderate / Comfortable Middle of the road on both difficulty and results. No action needed, but there is room to push further.
Not enough data yet This teacher has no assignments with difficulty data recorded. Usually a new teacher. No action needed.

The 0–5 score under each verdict blends three measurements:

Measurement Plain meaning Where it comes from
Demand How much thinking the questions ask for — remembering a date is low, arguing a case is high. The thinking level recorded on each question, weighted by how many marks it carries.
Evidence Whether students were actually stretched. A class averaging 95% was not. Average score achieved across all graded work. Needs at least 5 graded submissions.
Standards Whether essay-type questions say what a good answer looks like. Share of open-ended questions that carry a marking rubric.

Use the verdict, not the number, when comparing teachers. A teacher who sets harder questions but marks generously can score lower than one who sets easier questions and marks honestly — the verdict tells those two apart, the number does not.
